My research begins with a recurring inefficiency in aquaculture: valuable feed nutrients leave production as dissolved wastes and sludge. I study how fish, crops, microbes and algae can keep these resources in use. Linking fish health, food quality and the condition of receiving waters gives this work a practical One Health focus, done with global partners.

The first thread, advanced aquaculture and the circular bioeconomy, gives each waste stream its next job. Zero discharge RAS with novel denitrification units keep the water in use. Single-stage aerobic biofloc captures N and P, and microaerophilic reactors turn fish waste into microbial protein. Multiloop aquaponics with anaerobic digestion or biochar passes the rest to crops and biogas. Microalgal photobioreactors provide another route to nutrient capture and useful biomass.

The other thread, smart aquaculture and AI, makes these systems measurable, predictable and manageable. Dynamic models built on mass and energy balances, tested across contrasting climates, show where integrated production stays efficient as conditions shift. Sensing and imaging read fish behavior and physiology, flag stress and rising oxygen demand early, and set feeding by evidence rather than habit, all without netting a single fish. Digital twins update these models with live sensor data, estimate what no probe measures, and advise on feeding, aeration and dosing. The working rule is knowledge-guided AI: learning is welcome, but the mass balance still has to add up.
